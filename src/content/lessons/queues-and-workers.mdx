Gatepass decided the PDF, email and SMS can happen after the booking. Now someone has to do them, reliably, even when the web server that took the booking restarts a second later. The answer is a queue between the code that creates work and the code that does it, and a few numbers that tell you how many workers you need.

## Producers, a queue, consumers

Three roles, each with one job:

- **Producers** create jobs. The booking API is a producer: after committing a booking, it adds `send-ticket {bookingId: 9812}` to the queue and replies to the fan.
- **The queue** stores jobs durably until someone takes them. It survives restarts of the producers and the consumers.
- **Consumers**, usually called **workers**, are separate processes that loop forever: take a job, do it, report that it's done, take the next.

The queue buys you three things:

- **Decoupling.** The booking API doesn't know or care how tickets are delivered, how many workers exist, or whether the SMS provider is up.
- **Buffering.** When bookings arrive faster than workers can process them, jobs wait in the queue instead of being dropped or slowing the API down.
- **Independent scaling.** Add workers when the queue grows; the API servers don't change.

Common choices: a managed queue like Amazon SQS or Google Cloud Tasks, a Redis-backed library such as BullMQ or Sidekiq, RabbitMQ, or even a Postgres table using `SELECT … FOR UPDATE SKIP LOCKED` while volumes are small. They differ in features, but the model is the same.

## Taking a job safely: acknowledgements

What happens if a worker takes a job and then crashes halfway through sending the email? If taking the job removed it from the queue, the job is lost.

So queues don't delete a job when it's handed out. They hide it. The worker gets the job with a **visibility timeout** (or lease), say 30 seconds. When the worker finishes, it **acknowledges** the job and the queue deletes it. If the worker crashes, or takes longer than 30 seconds, the job becomes visible again and another worker picks it up.

This gives you **at-least-once** delivery: every job is done at least once, and occasionally twice. A worker that sent the email and then crashed before acknowledging will cause a second email. You can't avoid that with a better queue. You handle it by making jobs safe to repeat, which is the idempotency lesson.

<KeyIdea>A queue guarantees a job won't be lost, not that it runs exactly once. Every job you write must be safe to run twice.</KeyIdea>

## How many workers? Do the arithmetic

One worker that takes 250 ms per job finishes 4 jobs a second. Six workers finish 24 a second. That's your **capacity**. Compare it with the **arrival rate**:

- Arrivals below capacity: the queue stays near empty and jobs start almost immediately.
- Arrivals above capacity: the queue grows by the difference, every second, for as long as it lasts.

During a 30-second on-sale, bookings jump to 100 a second. With capacity 24 a second, the backlog grows by 76 jobs a second, reaching about 2,300 jobs. Once the rush ends and arrivals fall back to 20 a second, the queue drains at only 4 jobs a second, so it takes almost **10 minutes** to empty. The last fan in the rush waits roughly 2,300 ÷ 24 ≈ 95 seconds for their ticket, and anyone who books just after waits nearly as long.

Two rules of thumb fall out of that:

- **Wait time ≈ jobs ahead of you ÷ capacity.** A queue of 2,400 with capacity 24 a second is a 100-second wait. (This is Little's law in disguise.)
- **Run well below 100% utilisation.** The spare capacity is what drains a backlog. At 95% utilisation, a burst that took 30 seconds to build takes many minutes to clear.

Notice that a slow dependency and a traffic spike look identical from the queue's point of view: both lower the ratio of capacity to arrivals. That's why “queue depth” and “age of the oldest job” are the two metrics to alert on for every queue.

## Scaling workers on the queue

Because workers are separate processes, you can autoscale them on what actually matters: **backlog**, not CPU. A worker waiting four seconds for an SMS provider uses almost no CPU, so CPU-based autoscaling would never add workers while the queue explodes.

Scale on the age of the oldest job, or on depth divided by current capacity, and put a ceiling on it. More workers mean more concurrent calls to the SMS provider and more database connections. If the provider allows 50 requests a second, 200 workers just get rate-limited faster. The bottleneck has moved, not gone.

<Callout tone="tip" title="One queue per kind of work">
Put PDF, email and SMS jobs on separate queues with separate worker pools. If the SMS provider slows down, its queue backs up, but emails and PDFs keep flowing. A single shared queue lets the slowest job type hold up everything behind it.
</Callout>

## Key takeaways

- Producers add jobs, a durable queue holds them, and workers process them. None of them need to know about the others.
- Queues hand jobs out with a lease and delete them only on acknowledgement, so a crashed worker's job runs again. Expect at-least-once delivery.
- Capacity = workers × jobs per second each. When arrivals exceed capacity, the backlog grows by the difference every second.
- Wait time is roughly backlog ÷ capacity. Keep spare capacity so bursts drain in seconds, not minutes.
- Alert on queue depth and oldest-job age, scale workers on backlog, and separate queues by kind of work.
