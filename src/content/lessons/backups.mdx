On a Tuesday afternoon, a developer runs a cleanup script against what they believe is staging. It was production. By 3:14 pm, the `orders` table is empty, and the replicas, faithful as always, emptied themselves a few milliseconds later. Failover can't help and neither can replicas. The only thing between Gatepass and a very bad week is the backup, if one exists and if it works.

## What replicas and failover don't protect against

Everything so far protects against a machine dying. None of it protects against the data being wrong:

- a bad `DELETE` or `UPDATE`, or a migration that drops a column;
- a bug that quietly corrupts rows for three days;
- ransomware or a compromised credential;
- an entire region or cloud account being lost;
- someone deleting the database instance.

Replication copies the mistake. A backup is a separate, older copy that mistakes can't reach. They solve different problems, and you need both.

## Two kinds of backup

**Snapshots (full backups).** A copy of the whole database as of one moment. A nightly snapshot of a 200 GB database is easy to store and easy to restore. The weakness is the gap: restore last night's snapshot and every order since then is lost.

**Continuous log archiving.** The database already writes every change to a log (the write-ahead log in Postgres). Ship those log segments to storage as they're produced, typically every minute or so. Now you hold a base snapshot plus an unbroken stream of changes after it.

Together they give you **point-in-time recovery (PITR)**: restore the snapshot, then replay the log up to any chosen moment, such as 3:13:59 pm, one second before the script ran.

## RPO and RTO

Two numbers define what you need. Agree on them with the business before choosing tools.

- **RPO (recovery point objective)**: how much data you can afford to lose, measured in time. A nightly snapshot has an RPO of up to 24 hours. Log archiving every minute gives about 1 minute. Synchronous replication to another region gives close to zero.
- **RTO (recovery time objective)**: how long you can be down. Restoring 200 GB and replaying a day of logs may take 30 to 90 minutes. A warm standby takes seconds.

Tighter targets cost more. For Gatepass, orders and payments might need RPO under a minute and RTO under an hour. The marketing database can take RPO of a day and RTO of a day.

<KeyIdea>The backup you haven't restored isn't a backup. It's a hope.</KeyIdea>

## The 3-2-1 idea

A classic rule: keep **3** copies of the data, on **2** different kinds of storage, with **1** offsite. For a cloud database translate it as:

- the live database plus its replicas (copy one);
- automated snapshots and log archive in object storage (copy two);
- a copy in another region and, ideally, another account, with delete protection or object lock so a compromised credential cannot erase it (copy three).

Also **encrypt** backups and control who can read them. A backup holds all the data the database does, and it often has weaker access controls.

## Testing restores

Backups fail silently. Real incidents involve backups that were never configured on the new database, that ran but were empty, that were encrypted with a lost key, or that took 14 hours to restore when the business had four.

Make restores routine:

1. **Restore automatically every week** into a scratch instance, run a few sanity queries (row counts, the latest order timestamp) and alert if they fail.
2. **Time it.** The measured restore time is your real RTO, whatever the plan says.
3. **Practise the human parts.** Who decides? Where are the runbook and the credentials? Run a game day once or twice a year where the team restores to a chosen point without help.
4. **Monitor the backup itself.** Alert if the newest snapshot or log segment is older than your RPO.

<Callout tone="warn" title="Restoring over production">
When you recover from a bad delete, restore into a **new** instance first, then copy back only the damaged tables or rows. Restoring over the live database throws away the legitimate writes made since the mistake, and you can't undo it.
</Callout>

## Recovering from the bad script

For the 3:14 pm incident, the plan reads:

1. Stop the damage: revoke the script's access, put the app in read-only or maintenance mode.
2. Pick the target time, 3:13:59 pm, from the audit log.
3. Restore the last snapshot and replay logs to that time on a fresh instance. About 40 minutes.
4. Compare: export the lost `orders` rows from the restored instance and load them into production, then reconcile against payment-provider records for the orders placed in the gap.
5. Afterwards, fix the cause: separate credentials for staging and production, a confirmation on destructive scripts, and read-only access by default.

## Key takeaways

- Replicas and failover protect against hardware failure. Only backups protect against bad data, bugs and attackers.
- Snapshots plus archived logs give point-in-time recovery to any moment, such as just before a bad script.
- Choose RPO (data you can lose) and RTO (time you can be down) first. Tighter targets cost more.
- Keep copies in another region and account, encrypted and protected from deletion.
- Restore regularly and time it. An untested backup is only a hope.
